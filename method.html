Reading the map

A gauge measures the water at its own station, not at the swimming place it is linked to. Stage is the water level above the gauge’s reference point; it is not depth. The map no longer draws the gauges. Each place card names the gauge behind its color, with the reading, its time and the station’s flow history, and links to that gauge’s full record on the Hill Country water map wherever USGS keeps a daily one. Four gauges have none: Blanco River at Blanco reports water height only, and USGS returns no daily series for the Guadalupe at Guadalupe River State Park or the Frio at Rio Frio and above Concan.

How long a color lasts:

  • Creek flow: a USGS reading under 90 minutes old is current. An older one can stay on the map as a dated planning cue for up to six hours; the card gives its age.
  • Blunn Creek: a City Hydromet reading under 30 minutes old is current. An older green-band reading can stay a dated cue for at most two hours, and only when the three-hour flow-history check passes and the station reports no rain in six hours (details).
  • Austin pools and splash pads: a schedule color expires at the next boundary in its posted schedule or two hours after the oldest City page it relies on was checked, whichever comes first.
  • Other pools and lake beaches: a posted-hours color expires two hours after the operator’s page was re-read. Pages that only a browser can read are checked each morning, and those colors last 30 hours.

Green never means clean, safe, deep or permitted. Park shading is City parkland, not permission to swim. Creek lines are the City’s mapped channels, not proof of water. Official NWS notices, the heat and sun planning band and each station’s hydrologic context appear on the card only, never in the marker color.

Bull Creek flow band

The Bull Creek color is a provisional flow estimate. Its thresholds were drafted from three recorded visits and the USGS station’s historical high-flow threshold. The visit records are private, so this public copy does not include them.

At USGS station 08154700, the band reads: below 0.5 cfs red; 0.5–1.5 yellow; 1.5–10 green; 10–45 yellow; 45 cfs or higher red. The band has not been validated against independent visits. A reading at the station is not a measurement at either mapped swimming place.

Colors describe this flow band only. They do not establish water quality, depth, access, or safety. View the USGS station.

McKinney Lower Falls flow band

This provisional cue uses USGS 08159000. Below 1.19 cfs is red; 1.19–24.1 green; 24.1–258 yellow; 258 or more red. The two dated positive observations were near 4.6–5 cfs. The low red threshold is a conservative choice, not a measured depth threshold. A pool can retain water when the downstream station records no flow.

The station is about 4 km downstream of the falls, after Williamson Creek enters Onion Creek. Its reading combines both creeks, so the band has low confidence and has not been independently validated. TPWD rules and current notices govern access. View the USGS station.

Blunn Creek visit-reference flow band

Two reported good visits at the Big and Little Stacy reaches are treated as provisional green reference conditions. City Hydromet site 122, downstream on the same mapped creek, recorded 6 cfs during one reported visit window and 8–9 cfs during the other. The exact visit records remain private. This assumption supplies a flow cue for both reaches; it has no independent validation.

The map uses a small round-number buffer around those positive readings: 5–12 cfs green. Near-zero flow below 0.5 cfs is red, 0.5–5 cfs is yellow, and 12–22 cfs is yellow. Site 122 reports flow in whole cubic feet per second: of its 370,597 flow readings from November 13, 2015 through October 9, 2026, every value above zero is a whole number and the smallest is 1 cfs, and 46% are zero. A reading of 0 therefore covers any flow under about 1 cfs, including a visible trickle, and the 0.5 cfs line is finer than the station reports; in practice 0 is red and 1–4 cfs is yellow. Whether the City rounds or truncates is not established here, so 0 means either under 0.5 or under 1 cfs. At or above 22 cfs is red: 22 cfs rounds the station’s 95th-percentile daily mean in the captured 61-day May–June 2026 record (21.85 cfs). These yellow and red cutoffs are provisional choices, not observed swimming limits. A reading under 30 minutes old is labeled current. Green and yellow pause if one-hour station rain is positive or missing; a current red reading remains red. A failed source check withholds every cue.

An older green-band reading can remain a dated planning cue until two hours after the observation only when the official flow history passes a three-hour lookback check: a reading within 30 minutes of the window’s start, every reading in the 5–12 cfs band, no reporting gap longer than 30 minutes, and a history reading within 10 minutes of the latest station observation. The current City feed must also report zero rain at the station for six hours. Missing history or rain removes the cue. Yellow and red do not extend beyond 30 minutes. The map checks the City feed every 15 minutes while visible, so a newer reading replaces an earlier cue. The two-hour limit is a planning policy, not a forecast that flow will last two hours.

The City site 122 May–September 2026 flow record included an August 21 pulse: 5 cfs at 21:30 UTC fell to 0 by 23:45 UTC. That pulse failed the three-hour history check. In a replay of 4,577 overlapping favorable observations that passed the flow-history check, 11 were below 5 cfs about two hours later; none were zero. Those overlapping samples are not independent odds, and the replay did not include rain history. On October 1, the station also rose from 0 to 124 cfs in 15 minutes. A rise does not itself predict a rapid fall, and the station rain gauge cannot detect every upstream storm.

The gauge is about 1.5 km downstream of Big Stacy and 336 m downstream of Little Stacy. Its flow and stage do not measure depth, water quality, access, or safety at either reach. Austin warns that flash flooding can raise creek water quickly; check the separate official notice on each card. View the City Hydromet history.

Georgetown Blue Hole pool hypothesis

The City describes an upper Imhoff pool and a smaller downstream Kiddie pool, each held by a dam. The map draws separate approximate points inside Blue Hole Park. USGS 08104900 is upstream on the same South Fork and measures neither pool’s depth. One favorable park-level report is the only condition calibration; it does not identify which pool was used. This is a pale, hypothetical cue for each pool.

At the upstream station, below 50 cfs is pale yellow, 50–150 pale green, 150–500 pale yellow, and 500 cfs or more pale red. These are guesses, not observed thresholds. A rise of at least 30 cfs and 50% over about two hours changes pale green to pale yellow. Station stage at or above the NWS 7 ft action level makes the cue pale red. An active official flood or severe-weather warning makes it solid red; a watch, caution, or failed hazard check prevents green. A rapid rise is treated as changing flood risk, not proof that the pool will dry rapidly.

The reading is current for 90 minutes. Older readings up to six hours old remain dated planning context; an older green guess becomes yellow. A failed source check or a reading six hours old removes the color. The map refreshes USGS while visible. Neither a green guess nor a low-flow yellow guess establishes depth, water quality, access, or safety. The dams may hold water even with low upstream discharge. USGS station · NWS flood gauge.

Regional pale flow hypotheses

These pale colors guess how useful a station reading is for planning a visit. They do not rate swimming safety, depth, water quality, or permission. Each bracket is specific to its station and named reach. The exploratory brackets use approved USGS daily mean discharge for May–September 2016–2025 (1,530 days per station), plus the recorded McKinney Lower Falls reference. Historical frequency is a way to flag unusually low or high flow; it is not evidence that a middle-range flow makes a pool swimmable. The cutoffs below remain unvalidated hypotheses.

  • McKinney Upper Falls: downstream 08159000 reads Onion Creek plus Williamson Creek. Below 1.19 cfs is pale yellow; 1.19–24.1 pale green; 24.1–258 pale yellow; 258 or more pale red. Upper Falls lacks a dated, falls-specific favorable report, so its green bracket is a weak transfer from the Lower Falls record. A pool may retain water at zero flow.
  • Williamson Central Greenbelt: upstream 08158930: below 0.5 cfs pale yellow; 0.5–8 pale green; 8–85 pale yellow; 85 or more pale red. Williamson East Greenbelt: downstream 08158970: below 1 pale yellow; 1–26 pale green; 26–177 pale yellow; 177 or more pale red. These colors describe possible creek flow at park references. No swimming entry is verified at either marker. The unresolved Williamson Creek item stays off the map.
  • Jacob’s Well and Wimberley Blue Hole: 08170990 measures Jacob’s Well spring discharge. For Jacob’s Well (v2, checked against dated reports and County open seasons): below 1 cfs pale red; 1–2.5 pale yellow; 2.5–32 pale green; 32 or more pale yellow. For Wimberley Blue Hole: below 1 cfs pale yellow; 1–32 pale green; 32 or more pale yellow. These are separate place hypotheses using one source, not a shared depth claim. Wimberley says additional seeps feed Cypress Creek downstream, so zero at Jacob’s Well cannot establish that Blue Hole is dry. Hays County currently cancels Jacob’s Well swimming; Wimberley says Blue Hole swimming is closed until May 1, 2027. The map shows the operator closure ahead of any flow guess. The City reported Blue Hole swimming reopened June 26, 2025 after six days closed for storm-driven turbidity, while the approved daily mean at upstream Jacob’s Well was 0.60 cfs. That one paired date shows why the below-1-cfs yellow guess cannot be read as a dry or closed pool; it does not establish a green flow threshold. The Jacob’s Well closure check expires after seven days; the dated Blue Hole season check after 31 days. An expired check withholds a favorable color until reviewed.
  • San Marcos River at Sewell Park, City Park and Rio Vista: USGS 08170500 sits by Sewell Park upstream of the three mapped river points. Each has a separate pale hypothesis from the same upstream reading: below 110 cfs pale yellow; 110–350 pale green; 350–500 pale yellow; 500 or more pale red (bands checked against dated reports, v2). Texas State describes swimming stairs at Sewell; the City confirms river access at City Park and Rio Vista. Points come from the mapped river centerline beside those parks, not surveyed entries. One upstream flow reading does not measure depth or current at any of them; park access and notices remain separate.

For the hypotheses with a rapid-rise gate, a sufficiently large two-hour station rise changes pale green to pale yellow; it signals changing flood risk and does not predict drying. A fresh official NWS warning makes the marker solid red; caution or an unavailable warning check holds green at yellow. Readings under 90 minutes old are labeled current. A reading from 90 minutes to six hours old may supply dated planning context, with green reduced to yellow. After six hours, a failed source check, or an incompatible reading, the flow color disappears. The page uses its existing 15-minute visible-tab USGS check.

McKinney Lower Falls and Bull Creek District Park now display their existing provisional visit-reference bands as pale guesses. Their numerical brackets have not changed. Blunn Creek retains its separate City Hydromet rule. The Comal River Prince Solms card uses New Braunfels’ published recreation bands. Blanco’s nearby station reports stage only; the other uncovered parks have no supported local flow link or rule, so they remain unrated.

How to read the new flow history

Place and gauge cards with a linked discharge station offer 24-hour and seven-day plots of measured station flow in cfs. The map requests these only when a card is opened, reuses a station’s response across its linked places, shows the observation age, and leaves reporting gaps unconnected. The seven-day plot retains short peaks; it is not a daily-average line. A separate orange dashed line shows the latest checked station reading when its source check succeeds and the observation is still current. USGS history comes from the official continuous-data service, with its older instantaneous-values service as a fallback. Blunn’s plots come from City Hydromet site 122 through the existing same-origin source reader. They describe the station, not depth or safety at a swimming entry. Places without linked discharge say so.

The separate “Typical season” graph is a custom station reference in cfs, not an official climate normal or a swimming-quality range. Its line shows the historical median, its shaded band shows the middle half, and a vertical mark locates today. For each calendar date, the map takes a 15-day window centered on that date, computes one median of approved daily mean discharge per qualifying year, then summarizes those yearly values. USGS years require at least 330 approved daily values. City Hydromet site 122 uses daily means derived from at least 80 quarter-hour observations with no gap over 45 minutes; its qualifying years require at least 300 such days. A date needs at least ten USGS years or six City years. The card shows its actual date span and year count, and calls out a shorter record. The separate orange line is an instantaneous current station reading for visual context; it is not equivalent to the historical daily means. The historical panel never changes a green, yellow, or red place cue.
